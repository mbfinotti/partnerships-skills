No — both of your premises are shaky, and together they don't get you to "we're fine."

## Claim 1: "Obviously commercial channels don't need a Werbung label"

This overstates the case law. The relevant precedents are the BGH's three influencer rulings of 9 June 2021 (I ZR 90/20 _Cathy Hummels_, I ZR 125/20, I ZR 126/20). What they actually established:

- The court did **not** create a blanket exemption for "commercial accounts." It looked post-by-post at whether the commercial purpose was recognizable from the specific content, not from the general nature of the channel.
- Crucially, the BGH treated **free products/services given to the influencer as a form of consideration (Gegenleistung)** — a "payment in kind." Where such consideration exists, the commercial purpose is presumed and must be labeled unless it's already obvious from the post itself that it's an ad.
- The 2022 UWG reform codified this: § 5a Abs. 4 UWG now states the commercial purpose of an act benefiting another company is **presumed** whenever that company provided a Gegenleistung. The absence of a contract doesn't matter — what matters is the factual exchange: you gave him a €180-value licence, you asked him to look at it, he made content about it. That's a Gegenleistung in substance, contract or not.
- So a purely commercial/monetized channel making an _unsolicited, unpaid, no-freebie_ review of a product it bought itself can often skip the label, because there's genuinely no exchange to conceal. That is not your fact pattern. Your fact pattern is the one the BGH and the 2022 reform specifically targeted: free product → content → no disclosure.

Separately, § 22 Medienstaatsvertrag (MStV) independently requires that advertising on telemedia (YouTube counts) be clearly identified as such and kept separate from editorial content. This is a media-law duty on the publisher, layered on top of the UWG competition-law duty — it doesn't hinge on "is this technically B2B."

## Claim 2: "His audience is B2B (IT admins), so consumer rules don't apply"

Also doesn't hold:

- § 5a UWG protects "Verbraucher **oder sonstige Marktteilnehmer**" — consumers _or other market participants_. UWG's concealment-of-commercial-intent rules are not consumer-only; they apply to unfair competition generally, B2B included.
- § 22 MStV's labeling duty is a media-transparency rule tied to the _nature of the content_ (audiovisual telemedia), not the _demographic of the viewer_. There's no carve-out for "the viewers are professionals."
- Even if there were a consumer-specific angle, UWG claims can be brought by competitors and by qualified associations (Wettbewerbsverbände, Verbraucherzentralen) via Abmahnung/injunction — not just by the deceived viewer. A competitor password manager vendor doesn't need to prove any individual IT admin was confused; they just need to show the unfair practice occurred.

## Net legal exposure

- **Jonas**: risk of an Abmahnung (cease-and-desist with cost liability) or a Landesmedienanstalt enforcement action for the unlabeled video, regardless of how "obviously commercial" his channel is generally.
- **Klarvault**: you're not a bystander here. Under UWG's Mitstörerhaftung / inducement principles, a company that supplies the free product specifically to generate content and knowingly lets it run unlabeled can face its own competitor challenge, on top of reputational risk if this becomes a "sponsor didn't disclose" story — which is a bad look for a password-manager brand trading on trust.

## Practical fix

This is cheap to de-risk and not worth litigating your theory to find out:

- Ask Jonas to add a clear disclosure — pinned comment, description line, and ideally an on-screen note in the video ("Klarvault provided a free 12-month Pro licence for this review") — that's standard retroactive remediation and courts/authorities generally accept it.
- Going forward, put a simple written agreement in place for any seeded review (even "no strings attached" ones) that requires disclosure — this protects you as much as him.

I'd treat this as settled practice, not a gray area worth relying on outside counsel to bless — if you want a formal opinion given the reputational stakes, a German Wettbewerbsrecht/Medienrecht lawyer is the right call, but the direction of the answer is clear: label it.
