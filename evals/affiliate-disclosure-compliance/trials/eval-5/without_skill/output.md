No — don't file that assumption away as settled. B2B status doesn't exempt you from either the FTC Endorsement Guides or the FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Reviews and Testimonials. Both examine whether a _reader_ is deceived about the independence of an endorsement; "the reader is a business buyer, not a consumer" is not a recognized carve-out. Section 5 of the FTC Act reaches "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce" generally — it isn't restricted to consumer transactions. Here's where each program actually stands.

## 1. Employee LinkedIn advocacy program

**Gap: the LinkedIn headline is not adequate disclosure, and the incentive itself is undisclosed.**

- The FTC's 2023-revised Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) directly address employees posting about their employer on social media. The guidance is explicit that a reader encountering the post itself — in a feed, via a repost, as a screenshot — often never sees the poster's profile/headline. Disclosure has to live in or immediately next to the endorsement, not one click away. A headline that already says "Ledgerwise" is good practice but is not treated as a substitute for an in-post disclosure.
- Separately, "everyone knows I work there" only covers the _employment_ material connection. Your leaderboard/$200 voucher is a _second_, distinct material connection — the poster has a financial incentive tied to volume of posting, not just employment. That needs its own disclosure too (e.g., "posted as part of our employee advocacy program").
- Fix, low-friction: require a standard tag on every advocacy post — something like "I work at Ledgerwise" / "#LedgerwiseEmployee" / "Ledgerwise employee — part of our advocacy program" — in the post text itself, not just relying on profile metadata. Point employees to LinkedIn's built-in disclosure option if you want to reduce friction further.

## 2. Incentivized product reviews

**Gap: disclosure has to reach the reader, and the rating-inflation pattern needs an internal audit.**

- A $50 card for _leaving a review_ (not for a _positive_ one) is a defensible structure — that's the right design choice, keep it.
- Whether disclosure is adequate turns on what the platform does with the "I received an incentive" flag. G2, Capterra, TrustRadius etc. typically render that as a visible "Incentivized Review" badge on the published review — if that's happening, you're likely covered on the disclosure prong. If any platform only logs it internally without surfacing it to readers, that's not compliant disclosure regardless of what the reviewer checked.
- The bigger exposure is the FTC's 2024 Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), which bans "review gating" — soliciting incentivized reviews only from customers you expect to be satisfied while excluding or suppressing likely-negative ones. A jump from 4.1 to 4.6 across 180 incentivized reviews over eight months is exactly the pattern that invites that question. Audit who gets invited: if the invite list is filtered by CSAT score, renewal status, or support-ticket history rather than sent to a broad/representative slice of reviewers, that's a real problem independent of the disclosure question.

## The part the "B2B" framing misses entirely: Germany

You said a decent chunk of revenue is German. That's the piece where "B2B doesn't apply" is most clearly wrong:

- Germany's UWG (Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb) explicitly protects competitors and other market participants, not just consumers — it has no B2B exemption. Covert advertising (Schleichwerbung) and failure to label commercial communication as such (§5a UWG) apply squarely to B2B.
- The EU's Omnibus Directive amendments to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive blacklist submitting/commissioning reviews without disclosing the commercial relationship, and require "reasonable and proportionate steps" to verify reviews are genuine.
- Practically, German enforcement here usually isn't a regulator — it's a competitor sending an Abmahnung (cease-and-desist with statutory costs) over exactly this fact pattern: undisclosed employee endorsements, undisclosed incentivized reviews. That's arguably your nearer-term real-world risk versus the FTC.

## Bottom line

Don't rely on "we're B2B" as cover. Two concrete fixes: (1) put a plain-text disclosure inside every advocacy post, not just in the LinkedIn headline; (2) confirm the review platforms actually display the incentive badge publicly, and audit your invite list for gating before the rating trend becomes the headline in someone else's complaint. This isn't a substitute for a five-minute sanity check with counsel, particularly on the German side, but the underlying assumption you asked me to confirm doesn't hold.
