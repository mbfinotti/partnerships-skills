Here is the fix — not a new set of weights, but a different way of producing them, because the same weighting method will produce the same 90-minute fight in six weeks.

## What actually broke last time

Not the weights. The _method that produced them_ had no artifact the CRO could audit or contest on structured grounds — so the argument became opinion vs. opinion in the room, live, with no way to resolve it there. That's the documented failure mode of weighted scorecards: Dyer (1990) calls exactly this kind of unstructured weight-setting "arbitrary," and Belton & Gear (1983) show these rankings can flip just from adding or dropping one near-duplicate candidate. Individually-justified weights (a one-line rationale per weight) fixes this for most teams. It won't fix it for yours, because three separate conditions are all true at once here, and each one independently argues for going one rung further up the weighting-method ladder:

- The weights themselves are what the room fights about (not the candidates).
- The steering committee has already reopened one ranking.
- This scorecard runs at every portfolio review from now on — it has to survive repeated re-litigation, not just one meeting.

## The fix: elicit weights with the Best-Worst Method, with the CRO inside the room

Formal best-worst method (BWM) beats individually-justified weights on exactly the dimension you're stuck on — defensibility — at the cost of a half-day of structured elicitation. Given what's at stake (a second reopened FY plan, a repeating scorecard), that half-day is cheap. Prefer BWM over AHP: fewer pairwise comparisons, less rank-reversal exposure.

Run it like this:

1. **Fix the criteria set first**, before anyone touches a number. Use the complementarity-led default: resource complementarity, organizational compatibility, strategic-goal fit, JVP strength, alliance readiness, product complementarity vs. substitution, prior ties, market/ICP overlap — 8 criteria, no more. Write a one-sentence definition for each, so "integration depth" and "account overlap" are named, bounded terms before anyone weights them.
2. **Convene the actual voters** — CRO included, not just the analyst — for the elicitation session. Each participant privately names their _best_ (most important) and _worst_ (least important) criterion, then rates every other criterion against those two on a 1–9 scale. This produces a weight vector per person plus a consistency ratio — a number that flags if someone's own comparisons contradict each other.
3. **Aggregate across participants**, surface the consistency ratios, and reconcile any large disagreement in the session itself — not later, and not in the steering committee meeting.
4. **Anchor the two constructs with peer-reviewed support at the top regardless of what the vote produces**: complementarity and organizational compatibility (Sarkar et al.; Mindruta, Moeen & Agarwal 2016 — different in resources, similar in institutions). If the elicited weights put these below overlap or brand/logo criteria, that's a flag to re-run the session, not a result to ship.
5. **Cap account-overlap's weight explicitly**, and say why in the memo: overlap flags warm accounts, it doesn't predict alliance success — the category's own network data shows roughly an 11.7% win-rate lift, against a widely-quoted 53% _survey_ figure from the same vendor. Quote the smaller number, flag its provenance, and don't let it outweigh complementarity.

This directly ends the CRO's specific fight: "integration depth vs. account overlap" isn't a debate anymore, it's an input the CRO co-produced with a documented consistency check. If the CRO wants to argue about it in six weeks, they're arguing with their own pairwise comparisons from a session they sat in — not with your spreadsheet.

## Everything else stays the same, and stays outside the argument

- **Gates run staged, before scoring, and are never tradeable.** Financial viability, security/compliance, legal/antitrust, three-laws (Gomes-Casseres), exit path — desk checks across all 7 candidates first, expensive reviews (counsel, TPRM) only on survivors. A gate failure removes a candidate regardless of how it would have scored — no logo, no exec relationship, buys its way past a gate. This also means fewer candidates reach the room already carrying an unresolved gate flag, which was probably part of what made last quarter's meeting messy.
- **Score effort per candidate and rank on value ÷ effort**, not value alone — say that ordering explicitly in the memo, because the value column is what the room defaults to reading otherwise.
- **Score risk explicitly per candidate** (relational/performance per Das & Teng, plus co-innovation and platform-envelopment risk where relevant) and show it beside value and effort — not in an appendix nobody opens.
- **Set the capacity line on summed effort**, not just the raw "4 slots" headcount, once effort is scored — 7 candidates rarely cost the same manager-months apiece, and the effort-sum line is the only one that notices two cheap alliances aren't interchangeable with one expensive one. Fall back to the 4-slot headcount line only if the effort estimates turn out comparable.
- **Run the sensitivity analysis and put it in the memo**: shift every weight ±20%, one at a time, re-rank, and state which positions are stable and which flip. Treat any composite gap under ~5% as a tie, broken by documented judgment (goal fit, timing), never by decimals. This is the artifact that lets the CRO push on a specific weight and see, in the memo, exactly what it would take to change the outcome — instead of relitigating the whole table.

## Change how you present it, not just how you build it

Last time likely failed partly because a finished ranked table hit the room in one shot. Present the memo section by section, and get sign-off on each before moving to the next:

1. Gates and who failed them, with evidence.
2. Scorecard — criteria and weights, with the BWM consistency ratios attached.
3. Ranking — composite, effort, risk, side by side.
4. Sensitivity — what's stable, what flips.
5. Shortlist and verdicts (go / conditional-go / recycle / hold / kill), each with a first-value milestone and a re-entry trigger if not "go."

If the CRO already agrees the weights are legitimate by the time they see the ranking, there's nothing left to reopen except the ranking's _content_ — which is a much shorter, much more useful argument to have.

## Make this durable past this one meeting

Since this scorecard runs at every future portfolio review, don't re-elicit weights from scratch each time — that reopens the door every quarter.

- Record and keep the approved weight set, the BWM elicitation results, and every go/no-go verdict with its reasoning. Future runs start from this baseline.
- Only revisit weights on a named trigger — a new alliance type entering the mix, a criterion proving predictive or not, a material shift in strategy — not by default reopening.
- Set the portfolio review cadence now (quarterly or semi-annual) plus event triggers (a candidate gets acquired, a platform changes terms, a gate milestone slips) and put the date of the next review in this memo.
- Track a regret log — passed-over candidates who partnered with a competitor and visibly performed. It's the only honest check on whether the cut line was right, and it gives you evidence for or against the weights the _next_ time someone questions them.

## Six-week timeline

- **Week 1**: Confirm the criteria set and capacity approach with the sponsor. Widen/confirm the 7-candidate longlist.
- **Week 1–2**: Run staged gates across all 7; expensive reviews only on survivors.
- **Week 2**: Run the BWM elicitation session with the committee, CRO included. Resolve consistency-ratio disagreements in-session.
- **Week 3**: Score survivors on the fixed criteria/weights; score effort and risk per candidate.
- **Week 4**: Run the sensitivity analysis; draw the capacity line; draft verdicts and light business cases per shortlisted candidate.
- **Week 5**: Pre-socialize the memo section by section with the CRO 1:1 before the full committee sees it — no surprises in the room.
- **Week 6**: Present to the steering committee section by section; gate finalization on explicit sign-off.

The mechanism that stops the CRO from reopening this again isn't a better argument for your weights — it's that they aren't yours alone by the time the committee sees them.
