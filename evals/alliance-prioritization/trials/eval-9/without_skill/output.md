# Hanmere Group — Partnership Prioritization Memo

## 1. Reject the 15% deduction approach

Averaging Verrow Athletic's labor-risk exposure into its score as a flat 15% haircut is the wrong mechanism. Reasons:

- **It treats risk as fungible with value.** A pipeline-strength score and an unresolved labor-rights story measure different things — one is expected value, the other is tail/reputational risk. Blending them into one number hides the risk from anyone who only sees the final score.
- **It picks a magnitude with no basis.** Why 15% and not 40% or 5%? No diligence has scoped the actual exposure (litigation status, media trajectory, remediation timeline). A negotiated-feeling number is worse than no number.
- **It's asymmetric in a way that favors the biased party.** Your finance lead is optimizing to keep a high-pipeline deal alive, not to price risk. That's a legitimate commercial instinct, but it shouldn't set the risk methodology.
- **The exposure is disproportionate to the discount.** Hanmere is a consumer finance app — trust is the product. Co-branding with a partner mid-labor-controversy is a brand-trust risk, not a 15%-of-revenue risk. It could be reputationally binary (fine, or a real problem) rather than linear.

**Use a two-axis model instead: merit score (blind to the controversy) × independent risk gate (legal/comms, not finance).** Report both numbers side by side. Never collapse them into one.

## 2. Risk gate for Verrow Athletic specifically

Don't score around the issue — resolve or fence it:

1. Get legal/comms to classify status: unresolved-but-monitored, active litigation, or credible-and-worsening. This determines everything else.
2. If monitored/low-severity: proceed, but with contract terms that price the risk properly — a morality/reputational-harm exit clause, indemnification for claims tied to the labor story, and no joint public marketing until Verrow's own remediation is closed out.
3. If active or worsening: pause the co-brand conversation entirely. Highest pipeline contribution doesn't matter if the deal has to be unwound publicly six months in — that costs more than the pipeline is worth.
4. Either way, keep this decision with legal/comms as owner, not finance — finance has a conflict of interest here (they proposed diluting the very risk they'd be scored against).

## 3. Ostrander — partner, but use the build option as leverage

Five engineer-months to replicate their whole product is a strong BATNA. Use it as a negotiating lever, not a reason to walk away:

- Partner now for speed — rebuilding costs you the months you don't have.
- Negotiate as if you might not renew: short initial term (e.g. 6–9 months), pricing capped near what 5 engineer-months would cost you amortized, and a data/model portability clause so switching to in-house later isn't blocked.
- Set a calendar reminder at month 4–5 of the deal to re-run the build-vs-partner math with real usage data. Don't let the partnership become sticky by default.
- This is the lowest-effort item on the list — a vendor negotiation, not an alliance build. Budget it accordingly (see allocation below).

## 4. Bank-side tech alliances — treat as the core business, not a side bet

These plug directly into the risk-engine licensing line, which is a different (and arguably more central) business than the consumer app. They deserve the largest capacity share because:

- Bank sales cycles and compliance/security reviews are long and manager-heavy by nature.
- A stalled bank alliance doesn't fail quietly — it stalls your actual enterprise revenue line.
